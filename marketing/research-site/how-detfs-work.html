1. The problem

People want ETF-shaped products: one transferable share over a basket, without hiring a discretionary portfolio manager. Onchain attempts often fail in familiar ways: opaque rebalancers, a “NAV” that disagrees with the market, nested vaults that freeze prices while underlyings move, or admin hands that can rewrite policy after you enter.

A DETF (Decentralized ETF) answers with a different product shape: the share is onchain, the reserve is real and inspectable, mint and burn rules are priced from that same reserve, and instances are not rewritten by an owner after launch.

This is not a registered securities ETF. “Decentralized ETF” is product language for basket-like reserve + share — not an SEC fund, and not legal ownership of offchain stocks or bonds.

2. What a DETF is (one screen)

  • One onchain share token — the unit you hold, transfer, and trade when markets exist.
  • A real multi-asset reserve — the pool of assets (and strategy vault shares) that backs and prices the unit.
  • A bond path — capital that opens a new unit and deepens shared reserve depth over time.
  • Mint and burn — primary market exchange into or out of the DETF against the reserve, under rules fixed at deploy.

Pricing is not a private spreadsheet. Mint, burn, and the reserve-based price of the unit share one engine: the reserve itself.

4. Lifecycle: closed → open → live use

State What you can do Reserve
Closed Primary mint and burn are blocked Not yet live as a product market
Live (after first successful bond) Mint and burn follow Policy or Open rules Shared depth established; more bonds can deepen it

The first bond is special: it opens the unit. Waiting for time to pass does not open a closed unit by itself.

Typical user steps after open:

  1. Bond — add locked capital / deepen shared reserve under onchain terms.
  2. Mint or burn — move between strategy vault shares and DETF when rules allow.
  3. Hold, claim, or trade — free share, bond rewards, or secondary market when available.

5. Policy vs Open

Every DETF chooses a mint/burn mode at deploy. Mode is explicit — it is not inferred from zeroed numbers.

Policy (default) Open
Mint when live Only when the unit is rich vs its reserve Anytime (product routes still apply)
Burn when live Only when the unit is cheap vs its reserve Anytime
Quiet middle band Neither mint nor burn on the primary market (secondary/reserve still exist) No price quiet band for primary mint/burn
Common defaults About +5% rich / −5% cheap vs an abstract 1.0 reference Stored for reference; price limits are not applied
Extra supply over time Possible when rich — to bond holders only Never
Peg or return promise? No No

Policy is for units that want primary mint and burn to respect richness vs the reserve. Open removes those price restrictions only — fees can still apply; it is not freer yield and it does not run natural expansion.

6. Where value can come from (honest)

Be clear about sources of economic exposure — without inventing APY:

  • Reserve composition — which assets and strategy legs sit in the basket, and how they are weighted.
  • Bonding — participation in shared reserve depth under published terms.
  • Fees — usage and related splits when the product and fee settings apply them (sizes are not guarantees).
  • Secondary markets — trading the DETF share when liquidity exists.

Do not expect: automatic yield, rebase “return,” locked APY, or “always above peg.”

7. Bond rewards vs free holders

Three paths are easy to confuse. Keep them separate:

Path Plain meaning
Capital mint External capital (for example strategy vault shares) mints DETF when rules allow. Fees and inventory splits may credit bond reward books — this is not “expansion.”
Extra supply over time Only under Policy, when the unit is live and rich. New free DETF accrues to bond holders. Free unlocked holders get none. Open never does this.
Protocol reserve deepening Protocol-owned bond rewards can sink into more shared reserve over time. That can improve long-run backing for claim-style paths when wired — it is not a coupon you cash at a fixed rate.

8. How the reserve stays honest

The product story depends on marks that track reality. Nested strategy vaults inside a multi-asset reserve need a way to re-mark when underlyings move. When re-marking is on, mid prices track demand better. When it is off, mids can lag while the world moves.

Lag is a fairness issue. It is not free profit. Closing a gap still has to clear pool fees, path costs, and impact. Modest lag often never pays.

On supported capital mint paths, the product aims for what you were quoted is what executed. That is a trust bar for primary market honesty — not a performance claim.

9. Types of DETF you can create

Several designs share the same DETF pattern. Only the reserve shape changes. Use plain labels — not internal package names — when choosing:

Type Choose when
Single strategy basket Exactly one strategy vault plus a DETF reserve
Multi-strategy weighted Several strategies that must keep distinct weights/valuations
Stable multi-strategy Like-kind or similar-rate targets composed together
Mixed buffer Shared buffer asset as the common rate target; burns return buffer only

Measured empirics in the technical note focus on a single-strategy composition first. Other types follow the same product law as they are proven in public.

10. What we verified

Controlled runs (not mainnet portfolio performance) support these product statements:

  • Units start closed; first bond opens them; time alone does not.
  • Policy rich/cheap bands behave as described; Open has no price limits when live.
  • Open does not run extra supply expansion over long idle periods.
  • Policy expansion, when it runs, credits bond books — not free unlocked holders.
  • Quote matched execution on the measured capital mint path.
  • Protocol rewards can deepen shared reserve principal over time.
  • Nested vault marks stay aligned when re-marking is wired; lag is not free arb after fees.

Honest limitation: after first bond, a unit may sit cheap vs default Policy bands. Making it rich enough to mint can require real inventory activity — not only trading the underlying pool. That is a book-structure observation, not a license to fake prices.

11. Risks and non-claims

  1. Not a registered securities ETF — no legal ownership of offchain underlyings.
  2. No peg, APY, or coupon guarantee — Policy bands and expansion are mechanisms, not promises.
  3. Open removes price limits only — not fees, not returns, not expansion.
  4. Market risk remains — inventory risk, volatility, and adverse selection on underlying legs still apply.
  5. Immutable units — misconfigured launches are abandoned, not silently rescued by an admin.
  6. Controlled evidence only — measured results are not mainnet TVL, APY, or volume guarantees.
  7. Eligibility — some underlying tokens may be restricted by issuer or venue rules; users own compliance.

12. What’s next

Public path: product education first (this report), then deploy addresses and app surfaces, then markets. Chain order remains Robinhood Chain first, then Base and Ethereum, then more EVM homes as markets mature.
